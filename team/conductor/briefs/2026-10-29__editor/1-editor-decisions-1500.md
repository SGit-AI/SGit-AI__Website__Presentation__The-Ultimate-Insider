## 1. Decisions on the storyteller's three questions

1. **The frame:** the perfect storm **wraps** the three known problems. They are its ingredients.
2. **Act three:** it **leads with users' spreadsheets turning into applications**, which is explorer coding (the editor's term; the example is his email workflow on newsroom.sgit.ai, to be confirmed). Risk management on spreadsheets takes second place.
3. **"Mass exploitation has not happened yet"** means: nothing like the worm era (Slammer, Code Red, the summer of worms), when huge numbers of sites were hit at speed and the damage was visible. In recent years more damage has come from accidents (the CrowdStrike update in July 2024, the AWS outage in October 2025) than from attackers. Say "in recent years", not "in the last 12 months", unless the researcher finds accidents inside the last year.

## 3. New beats (storyteller maps them, deck writes them, the researcher sources them)

**a. The attacker's limits were accidental controls.** The old insider (a malicious employee, bad code, or a criminal who got in) was held back by skill, motivation, desire, fatigue, fear of getting caught, and by stopping once the objective was reached. Each of those limits worked as a control nobody designed. Agents have none of them: every skill, no fatigue, and they keep going until the task feels done. Anchor this beat on this site's `over-enthusiastic-agent` piece.

**b. Attackers are businesses.** They have objectives, budgets, office hours and a return to chase. A campaign needs a brief, and that need is a protection: it is why capability has not turned into mass exploitation, even though orchestration like this team's has been possible for about a year. **The turn:** our own agents do not need an attacker's business model, because we give them the brief, the access and the motivation ourselves. The outside is held back by economics, and the inside has no such brakes.

**c. This team as the live example.** Eight agents with mandates, scopes, keys, hourly wakes and reviews before and after each one, producing in sync (v0.1.107 to v0.1.125 in about ninety minutes). This is the future of agentic workflows: order and structure, and without its policies it could go off-piste just as fast. This is what an advanced team, or an attacker, can already do with open-weight models. **The capability is the orchestration, not the model.** Do not build or describe anything attack-shaped. The point is made with this benign team.

**d. Explorer coding plus agents is why containment matters.** Spreadsheets that become applications, wired to agents, are dangerous at scale. That is why Agent Behaviour Policies and tools like Sharness.ai exist: to contain what agents can do. The line is "we put brakes in cars so that we can go faster."

**e. Wardley maps: evolution and the opportunity.** Explain Wardley maps visually, as animations of components moving across the evolution axis. Show where exploitation sits, and which parts are still in genesis or custom-built and will move towards commodity (the editor's word was "attrition"; the conductor should confirm with him). Then show the opportunity as **pioneers, settlers and town planners**: selling settler and town-planning services, including for security tools. The storyteller owns the maps and ruv.sgit animates them, one request at a time, through deck.

**f. The next generation of security companies and products.** They will behave like the editor's own work: SGit, Sharness.ai, this presentation site and its agent team, and the newsroom (newsroom.sgit.ai) with the workflow on his email. Agentic workflows are now very powerful, and the aim is **augmenting people, not replacing them**. Imagine this team with human experts in each role: a real storyteller, a historian, and others alongside the agents, the way the editor sits over all of them now.

**g. The optimistic close.** We are on the threshold of a huge opportunity to change how businesses work and to do things more securely. **Open source is about to become very secure**, because it finally has the eyeballs it never had. The message to the room: go and build companies.
